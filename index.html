Theory & FAQ

The planes, the P system, and what to look for
What's the difference between the Delivery Plane and Hogan's Glass?

They are two different reference surfaces answering two different questions.

The Delivery Plane (teal, ~48°) passes through the ball at roughly the angle of the shaft at address. It answers: where should the club travel as it approaches and strikes the ball? Through P5–P8 the shaft and clubhead ride close to this surface — that stretch is what launch monitors and coaches usually mean when they measure "swing plane."

Hogan's Glass (blue, ~62°) is the pane Ben Hogan described in Five Lessons: a sheet of glass leaning from the ball up through your own shoulders, resting on the neck, with your head poking through a hole. It's computed here from this golfer's actual build — a taller or more bent-over player would tilt it differently. It answers a different question: how steep is too steep on the backswing? It's a ceiling, not a rail.

So: backswing stays under the glass; delivery happens on the lower plane. The gap between them is where the downswing "shallows."

Does everything really have to stay under the glass?

On the backswing, yes — but with a nuance most people miss. Hogan was describing his lead arm, not the clubshaft. His instruction: the lead arm stays below the glass for the entire backswing, and at the top (P4) the back of the lead forearm should feel like it brushes the underside of the pane. Shattering the glass going back means your arms lifted too steeply.

In practice Hogan's own arm never came close to the pane — it's a limit that guards against steepness, not a target to swing along. And after impact the club naturally exits up past where the glass would be; the pane concept only governs the backswing.

What is "the slot"? People say "you're perfectly in the slot."

The slot is the space between the two planes — and being "in the slot" means your transition dropped the club into it. Hogan described the downswing happening on a second, shallower plane: when the hips fire first (P5), the trail shoulder drops and the whole plane tilts to point slightly right of the target. The club falls from the backswing plane down onto the delivery plane — under the glass, behind your hands, attacking the ball from the inside.

Scrub P4 → P6 from Down the Line and watch the club drop beneath the backswing path: that drop is the slot. The opposite move — the club going above the plane in transition — is "over the top," the classic slice move.

What do the P1–P10 positions actually mean?

The P Classification System splits the swing into 10 checkpoints, each defined by geometry, so any two swings can be compared frame to frame.

P10End of swingFull wrapPlanes no longer apply — balance is the checkpoint

Notice the symmetry: P8 mirrors P2, P9 mirrors P3. The downswing positions look like the backswing ones but sit deeper and shallower — that difference is the loop you see in the club path.

Why is the club path a loop instead of a circle?

Because the backswing and downswing happen on different planes. Going back, the club sweeps wide and then climbs as the wrists hinge (narrowing the arc). In transition it drops onto the shallower plane — so it comes down inside the path it went up on. Through impact the arc is widest again, and the finish wraps it around the body. Trace ≠ circle; trace = tilted loop. The takeaway (P2) and the delivery (P6) look similar in a photo, but the P6 club is closer to your body line — compare their markers from Down the Line.

What does the "lead arm – shaft angle" number mean?

It's the angle between the lead arm and the clubshaft — the classic measure of wrist hinge. Around 180° the arm and club form one straight line (fully released). Around 90° the wrists are fully hinged. The swing's whole story lives in this number: ~150° at address, gradually setting to ~80–90° at the top, held through P5–P6 (that's lag), then released back to ~150° at impact. Note P6 shows a smaller angle than its mirror position P3 — more stored angle on the way down than at the same spot going up. That difference is stored speed.

What is lag, and why does everyone obsess over it?

Lag is retained wrist hinge in the downswing — the clubhead trailing behind the hands while the body rotates. It matters because releasing the angle late means the clubhead is still accelerating at the ball instead of before it. The killer fault is casting: throwing the angle away from the top, so the arm–shaft angle is already ~150° by P6 and there's nothing left to release. Scrub P5 → P7 at ¼× and watch the angle number: it should stay low through P6 and snap open only through impact.

What would "over the top" look like in this viewer?

Over the top is the anti-slot: instead of the club dropping under the backswing plane in transition, the trail shoulder spins out and throws the club above it — through Hogan's glass on the way down. The club then approaches from outside the target line and cuts across the ball (out-to-in), producing pulls and slices. In this model the P5–P6 club stays below and inside the backswing path; an over-the-top move would show the downswing trace crossing outside the backswing trace.

Some videos draw THREE planes — shaft, elbow, and shoulder. How do those map here?

Swing analysts often draw three reference lines from Down the Line: the shaft plane (through the sweet spot and the hands at address), the trail-elbow plane (sweet spot through the trail elbow), and the trail-shoulder plane (drawn at the top, through the ball and the trail shoulder). The insight is that the backswing gradually steepens through them: the club traces the shaft plane at P2, sits between the elbow and shoulder planes at P3, and reaches its steepest — near the shoulder plane — at P4. Steeper than the shoulder plane at the top usually predicts an over-the-top move.

Our two panes bracket that progression: the Delivery Plane is essentially the shaft plane, and Hogan's Glass (ball through the shoulders) is essentially the trail-shoulder plane. A "neutral" top traces the shoulder plane; this model swings slightly under it — the flatter, trail-elbow-tucked look of Hogan and Jason Dufner. Above it (Jim Furyk) works too, as long as the ~90° relationship between the two arms is kept.

Related driver detail: with a full ~90° shoulder turn, a longer club on a flatter plane reaches parallel at the top; shorter clubs on steeper planes stop short of parallel. Past parallel, the club points right of the target; short of it, left — pure inclined-plane geometry, not a fault.

Why does the downswing plane point right of the target?

Because the body moves toward the target while rotating. Hogan: starting the downswing with the hips shifts everything left (for a right-hander), lowers the trail shoulder, and tilts the plane so it aims slightly right of the target. Swinging along that tilted plane produces the in-to-out attack that squares the face with speed instead of manipulation. It's not something you aim — it's the geometry that falls out of a hips-first sequence.
